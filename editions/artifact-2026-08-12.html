The One Thing

Yesterday's edition led on a market "pricing a ~55% chance the Fed hikes in September" and told you I'd fade that hike. Today is the day the data adjudicates it — and the early tape is already leaning my way, even as oil refuses to cooperate.

This is twin-CPI day, and it is the single most consequential Wednesday of the quarter for your book. India's July print lands at 4:00pm IST (Street ~4.5% vs June's 4.38%); the US July print lands tonight around 6:00pm IST, with the whisper now firmly toward cooling — some early wires already floating a ~3.2% headline against a 3.4% consensus, core sticky near 4.7%. If that holds, yesterday's "55% September hike" pricing was the top of a fear that is now being unwound before your eyes, and the Warsh Fed's hawkish rhetoric meets a labour market that just printed −23,000. But — and this is the whole tension — oil went the wrong way overnight. Brent pushed to ~$89.5, a hair below $90, on the same Iran/Hormuz impasse, so the disinflation the US CPI shows in July is being contradicted in real time by the barrel that sets August's number. You are being asked to buy a disinflation story with the primary inflation input rising. That contradiction is the day.

Layered on top of the macro is a genuinely rare India-idiosyncratic shock: reports overnight that Tata Sons chairman N. Chandrasekaran has discussed stepping down ahead of the 18 August AGM, amid an escalating governance war with Tata Trusts over board seats, strategy and the Shapoorji Pallonji exit. This is a governance event sitting over roughly a fifth of India's listed market cap — TCS, Tata Motors, Titan, Trent, Tata Power, Tata Steel — and it is the kind of promoter-level uncertainty the market has not had to price in the Tata complex for a decade. US indices closed lower Tuesday (S&P −0.32% to 7,728, Nasdaq −0.60%, Alphabet sank), so there is no overnight beta tailwind either. Net: a nervy, flat-to-firm open (GIFT ~24,561), two data bombs after the bell, and a Tata overhang. Below: what changed, where I still break with the crowd, and how to be positioned into 4pm.

The dovish crowd is about to over-celebrate a soft US CPI — the real message is "no hike, no cut," which is worse for the India bull case than people think. If tonight prints ~3.2% headline, the tape will treat it as a green light and rip. But look at core near 4.7% with Brent at $90: that is not a number that lets the Warsh Fed cut. The regime that actually resolves out of this week is higher-for-longer, both-ways-blocked — and that is precisely the environment that doesn't deliver the dovish DM pivot I've been arguing is the catalyst to pull FII money back to India. The trade is not "own duration for the cut"; it is "own the domestic-earnings compounders that don't need the Fed" — the banks, the autos, the GST-levered consumption names — and stop waiting for a rate-cut rescue that a $90 barrel has taken off the table. Where I'd be wrong: if oil rolls under $80 and core cracks below 4.5% next month, the pivot is back on and I'll chase it. Neither has happened.

The backdrop: a deepening clash between Tata Sons and Tata Trusts over board representation, group strategy, and how to manage the Shapoorji Pallonji minority exit — the same fault line that has periodically threatened the group's governance since 2016. His chairman term runs to February 2027, so this is not a scheduled succession; it is friction breaking into the open. Read-through: the operating businesses — TCS, Tata Motors, Titan, Trent, Tata Power, Tata Steel — are unaffected in fundamentals, but the market will (correctly) apply a small governance/holding-company discount across the complex until the AGM resolves, with TCS and the holdco-proxy names most exposed to headline risk. My take: this is 90% noise for the underlying equities and 100% signal for the SP-stake and any eventual Tata Sons listing question — the real prize buried in this fight is whether pressure to monetise pushes Tata Sons itself toward a listing, which would be one of the largest events in Indian capital-markets history. Watch the 18 Aug vote; fade any 3–5% knee-jerk selloff in TCS/Titan on quality operating businesses, but respect that the overhang caps upside near-term.

Pattern worth naming

Two supply floods are now visibly converging on the same domestic bid — and one of them is a promoter fight, not a choice. Yesterday I flagged the IPO pipeline (Shiprocket → Zepto → Jio) as the mechanism that eventually tops out a strong secondary market by diverting SIP flows into primary paper. The Tata Sons governance war adds a second, rarer channel: forced monetisation. The Shapoorji Pallonji exit needs liquidity, and the cleanest way to create it is a Tata Sons listing or a large stake sale — potentially the biggest single supply event India has ever seen. The pattern to watch is not any one IPO's subscription number; it's whether the aggregate paper being pushed toward the domestic bid — voluntary IPOs plus involuntary Tata-complex monetisation — starts to exceed even the SIP machine's ~₹27,000 cr/month absorption capacity. That, not a sentiment break, is still the most likely way this resilient market finally rolls over. File the Tata fight under "supply," not just "governance."

A soft US CPI tonight that ignites a risk rally would, on this logic, be fading a fresh oil-driven inflation impulse — which is exactly why I distrust an unqualified dovish celebration.

Rupee transmission: the dollar channel is bullish INR, the oil-plus-gold channel is bearish INR, and the second is winning. USD/INR at 95.4 with a sub-100 DXY is the whole macro story in one cross — the rupee should be stronger and isn't, because the terms-of-trade shock is real.

06Opinions Worth Reading

On the twin-CPI setup. The most useful framing floating through strategist notes this week is the distinction between a demand-pull and a cost-push inflation scare, and why the Warsh Fed's reaction function is mis-calibrated to the current one. The argument I find persuasive: a central bank hiking into an oil-driven price spike is treating a supply shock as if it were an overheating economy, and the −23k payroll print is the market's evidence that it isn't. If you read one thing today, read anything that forces you to separate "inflation is high" from "inflation is high for a reason monetary policy can address" — that distinction is the entire trade tonight, and it's the seed of today's Concept.

On the AI-financing question (carried forward). Last week's Bloomberg Opinion piece — the shift in the AI worry from valuation to financing, as hyperscalers move from cash-funded to debt-funded capex near $725bn — got fresh support Tuesday from Alphabet's slide and the market's continued willingness to punish capex-without-visible-return. The Korean chip-export number (+155% YoY) is the bull's rebuttal: the demand pulling silicon is real, not vapour. Hold both thoughts — real demand and dangerous financing are not mutually exclusive, and the second is what turns a correction into a crisis.

On Indian governance. The Tata Sons–Trusts standoff deserves the commentariat's attention less as a personality story and more as a case study in the promoter-holdco discount that Indian minority shareholders perennially underprice. When control and cash-flow rights diverge — a charitable trust controlling an operating empire — the governance risk is structural, not episodic. Worth reading anyone who treats the AGM not as gossip but as a referendum on how India's largest business house resolves the control-vs-liquidity tension that the SP exit has forced into the open.

09Concept of the Day

The Reaction Function

On a day when two inflation prints will move your book, the concept that actually matters isn't inflation — it's the reaction function: the (usually unstated) rule by which a central bank maps incoming data into policy. You already know the truism "it's not the news, it's the news versus expectations." The reaction function is the deeper version: a data point only matters through the lens of how the policymaker will respond to it, and that lens has weights you have to infer. A 4.7% core CPI is hawkish under a Fed that weights price stability at 100%; it is close to irrelevant under a Fed whose weight has just shifted toward employment because payrolls went negative. Same number, opposite trade — and the difference is entirely the reaction function.

Watch it govern today in stereo. The RBI's reaction function right now weights inflation heavily and is explicitly data-dependent — Governor Malhotra wants "greater clarity" before moving, and is draining liquidity via VRRR — which is why a 4.4% vs 4.6% India print is a genuine fork: it's the exact input his stated rule is waiting on. The Fed's reaction function is mid-shift and contested: Warsh's rhetoric says price-stability-first, but a 9–3 board and a −23k payroll are dragging the effective weight toward the labour mandate faster than the podium admits. When a reaction function is in transition — as the Fed's is — volatility is highest, because the market can't agree on which rule is being applied, so every print gets traded twice: once for the number, once for what it implies about the rule itself.

Application to your book. Before tonight, don't ask "will CPI be high or low?" Ask "what does each central bank do with a high or low number, given where its reaction function is right now?" That reframing tells you the India downside surprise (≤4.4%) is more tradable than the US one, because the RBI's rule is stable and waiting on exactly that variable, while the Fed's rule is already in motion regardless of the print. It also tells you why I keep separating "fade the hike" from "fade the pivot": the hike fades because the Fed's weight is shifting toward jobs; the pivot fades because the level (core 4.7%, oil $90) still fails the price-stability test even under the new weights. Trade the rule, not the number — and size up only when a print lands squarely on the variable a stable reaction function is waiting for.
